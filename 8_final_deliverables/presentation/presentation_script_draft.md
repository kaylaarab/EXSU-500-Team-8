| 0:00-0:30 | 1. Team | all four, one sentence each | "I'm [name]..., [role]." Then: "We are Team 8, and we built a leak-free evaluation of 30-day readmission prediction for diabetic patients." | team names, course, title |
| 0:30-1:10 | 2. Problem | [A] | "About 1 in 9 diabetic hospital stays ends in a readmission within 30 days. It is a standard quality measure and costly, so ranking patients at discharge could help target follow-up. The task: from information known at discharge, predict whether a patient comes back within 30 days." | one stat; task statement |
| 1:10-2:00 | 3. Data | [B] | "We used the public Diabetes 130-US Hospitals data: 101,766 stays from 130 hospitals, 1999 to 2008. We removed 2,423 stays that ended in death or hospice, which cannot be readmitted, leaving 99,343 stays, 11.4% readmitted. Two problems: many patients appear several times, and the data lack labs, social factors and follow-up plans." | missingness bar chart; patient/row split diagram |
| 2:00-2:45 | 4. Protocol | [C] | "To avoid inflated scores we split by patient, so nobody is in both training and test, tuned only on validation, and scored the test set once. Because only 11% are readmitted, we always report accuracy together with sensitivity: a model that says 'no' to everyone has 89% accuracy and catches nobody." | split diagram; the 89% vs 0% example |
| 2:45-3:45 | 5. Models and results | [D] | "We compared a baseline, logistic regression, XGBoost, CatBoost, a tuned neural network and an ensemble. All land between 0.66 and 0.68 AUROC; CatBoost is best at 0.677, a statistical tie with the rest. The standard clinical score, LACE, gets 0.58, so machine learning adds about 0.10. Sensitivity is about 55 to 60%, precision about 1 in 6." | Table I; LACE row highlighted |
| 3:45-4:30 | 6. What did not help | [A] | "After the midterm, we tried a true stacked meta-learner and a wider hyperparameter search for CatBoost, on top of synthetic oversampling, LACE and comorbidity features, three ways to use the later-readmission label, entity embeddings, and stronger tabular deep networks. None of it moved performance beyond statistical noise -- the stacking and wider search looked promising at first, but didn't hold up once we re-checked them, and we report that honestly rather than keep the bigger-looking number. Keeping payer and specialty and using prior-visit history gave the only small, real gains." | experiment log summary (`model_variations_log.md`) |
| 4:30-5:15 | 7. A cautionary result | [B] | "One published preprint reports 0.97 AUROC on this data. We reproduced that number with a plain model just by applying oversampling before cross-validation, 0.96. On real patients the same model scores 0.64 and catches 3% of readmissions. High scores here are evaluation artefacts." | EmbPred30 test table |
| 5:15-6:00 | 8. Trust and fairness | [C] | "After calibration, a 10% prediction means about 10%. But at our operating threshold the model catches 62% of women's readmissions versus 52% of men's -- confidence intervals that do not overlap, so this is a real, not a chance, gap -- and it works less well for patients over 70. Small racial groups cannot be assessed." | calibration curve; subgroup table (5_results/evaluation/subgroup_performance.csv) |

Limits and demo | [D] | "This is a research prototype — not a medical device, and not for any real clinical, administrative or payment decision: old data, performance near 0.68 that improves only slowly with more data, no external validation, and the sex-sensitivity gap we just showed still needs ethical review before any deployment-adjacent use. [If shown:] Here is our interface: it gives a calibrated risk, a band and a plain-language explanation." | limits list; interface screenshot or demo |
